# Draft comment letter — California State Bar, Proposed AI-Related RPC Amendments

**Two separate tracks — don't conflate them.** This letter targets the State
Bar's own rulemaking (RPC amendments), which has no confirmed hard deadline.
SB 574 is a separate, live **legislative** track: it passed the Senate 39-0 on
January 29, 2026, and the Assembly must act by **August 31, 2026** (five days
from today) or it dies for this session. A letter meant to influence the vote
has to go to Assembly members/the relevant committee, not the Bar — see
`sb-574-assembly-letter.md` for that separate, time-critical draft.

**Target:** Proposed amendments to California Rules of Professional Conduct 1.1,
1.4, 1.6, 3.3, 5.1, and 5.3 (including the new Comment to Rule 3.3 requiring
verification of AI-generated citations), approved for circulation by the Board
of Trustees May 14, 2026; and the "nondelegable" AI-verification duty in SB
574, as of the paper's writing still pending in the Assembly.

## Draft letter

To the Board of Trustees and the Committee on Professional Responsibility and Conduct,
The State Bar of California:

**Re: Proposed Amendments to the Rules of Professional Conduct Related to Artificial Intelligence (2026)**

I write to comment on the proposed amendments to Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.6, 3.3,
5.1, and 5.3, and in particular the new Comment to Rule 3.3 requiring
verification of AI-generated citations, together with the related
"nondelegable" verification duty proposed in SB 574.

I support a verification requirement calibrated to genuine risk. I write to
flag that, as currently framed, the requirement risks operating as a
facially neutral rule with a highly unequal practical effect, for reasons the
Bar's own data can test.

First, the empirical premise for treating unverified AI-sourced error as
categorically more serious than unverified human error is weaker than the
proposed Comment assumes. Human legal work is not error-free: independent
document-review teams in a widely cited study identified only 49% and 54% of
documents they should have produced, agreeing with each other in just 28% of
instances. Legal-specific AI research tools, as distinct from general-purpose
chatbots, now perform at or above human baselines on several measured tasks.
A verification standard should distinguish between tool architectures — a
closed-loop system grounded in a fixed set of verified authorities is not the
same risk category as an ungrounded general-purpose model — rather than
treating "AI-generated" as a single undifferentiated risk class.

Second, the Bar's own data suggests the burden of a uniform, high-intensity
verification mandate will not fall evenly. The Bar's 2025 Biennial Access,
Fairness, and Diversity Report already documents that discipline processes
fall more heavily on solo and small-firm practitioners, and that
thirty-nine percent of solo practitioners carry no malpractice insurance —
precisely the population with the least capacity to absorb an
hours-long, per-document re-verification requirement that large firms can
distribute across associate benches. A verification rule with no allowance
for tool type or proportionate risk will, in practice, tax exactly the
practitioners the Bar's diversity reporting identifies as already most
exposed.

I respectfully urge the Board to consider three modifications, consistent
with the Bar's own stated interest in accuracy and public protection:

1. **Calibrate verification duties to tool architecture and stakes**,
   rather than applying a uniform standard to every AI-assisted work
   product regardless of whether it comes from a general-purpose chatbot or
   a closed-loop legal research platform grounded in verified authority.
2. **Adopt a negligence, not "nondelegable"/strict, standard** for
   AI-sourced citation error absent actual subjective awareness of a
   specific risk — the same mental-state threshold the profession already
   applies to human-sourced error — rather than a standard that treats
   general awareness that AI can err as sufficient grounds for heightened
   liability.
3. **Pair any new verification Comment with a safe-harbor provision** for
   practitioners using tools that meet a defined reliability benchmark, so
   the rule rewards adoption of better tools rather than penalizing AI use
   as such.

I would welcome the opportunity to submit fuller written comments or
supporting research, including a working paper addressing the empirical and
doctrinal basis for these concerns in greater detail.

Respectfully submitted,
