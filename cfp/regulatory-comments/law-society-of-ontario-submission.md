## Draft submission

To the Law Society of Ontario:

**Re: Generative AI Guidance and the Distributional Effects of Verification Requirements**

I am writing to bring to the Law Society's attention research bearing
directly on the practical effect of verification obligations for
AI-assisted legal work, using the Law Society's own data.

The Law Society's *Statistical Snapshot of Lawyers in Ontario* (from the 2024
Lawyer Annual Report) documents that racialized and Indigenous lawyers are
concentrated in small-firm practice at markedly higher rates than white
lawyers: 24% of white lawyers practice in firms of fewer than five licensees,
compared with 37% of Indigenous lawyers, 33% of racialized lawyers overall,
and higher still for several specific groups — 46% of Southeast Asian, 39%
of Filipino, 37% of South Asian, and 35% of Black lawyers.

This distribution matters directly to how any AI-verification standard
operates in practice. A verification requirement that assumes a lawyer can
absorb hours of independent, traditional re-research for each AI-assisted
work product is far more costly, per lawyer, in a firm of one or two than in
a firm large enough to distribute that work across an associate bench. Given
the Law Society's own demographic data, a facially neutral verification
standard risks falling disproportionately on racialized and Indigenous
licensees — not because of anything in the standard's text, but because of
who is concentrated in the practice settings the standard burdens most.

I would ask the Law Society to consider, in any update to its generative AI
guidance:

1. **Whether verification obligations should be calibrated to tool type and
   matter stakes**, rather than applying uniformly regardless of whether the
   underlying tool is a general-purpose chatbot or a closed-loop legal
   research platform grounded in verified Canadian authority.
2. **Whether the Law Society's own demographic data supports monitoring the
   distributional effect of its guidance** — for example, tracking whether
   discipline or complaint outcomes involving AI use correlate with firm
   size or practice setting in ways that track the concentration data above.
3. **Whether proportionate, tiered guidance** (distinguishing, for instance,
   routine research support from dispositive filings) would better serve
   both accuracy and equitable access to the productivity gains AI tools can
   offer solo and small-firm licensees serving price-sensitive clients.

I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this further, or to provide the
fuller research paper this submission draws from.
