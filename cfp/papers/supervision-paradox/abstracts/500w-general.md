**Title:** The AI Supervision Paradox: Double Standards, Algorithm Aversion, and the Unequal Burden of Legal Tech Regulation

A solo practitioner in Denton County, Texas downloads a draft factum from a
generative AI system — comprehensive, correctly identifying the controlling statute,
citing three on-point appellate decisions, and costing less than five dollars in
tokens. Under her jurisdiction's standing order, she must now independently
"verify" every element of that factum using traditional, non-AI legal sources. That
verification takes as long as producing the factum without AI would have — the
mandate has multiplied the five-dollar factum's true cost by an order of magnitude.
Across town, a mid-level associate at a large firm receives the identical draft from
the identical model. She hands it to a junior associate to verify; the partner reviews
the result in minutes. The large firm has absorbed the verification burden by
distributing it across its pyramidal staffing structure. For the solo practitioner, the
same requirement is insurmountable: it reverses whatever productivity gain AI
offered and converts a technology that could have made her practice more
affordable into a compliance risk.

This paper argues that this disparity is not incidental. It is the predictable
consequence of a regulatory framework — visible in standing orders, bar guidance,
and a hardening "constructive recklessness" standard for AI-sourced citation error —
that treats AI errors categorically differently from human errors without empirical
justification for the distinction. The empirical record does not support that
distinction. Human document review is itself far from error-free: independent
reviewer teams in one influential study identified only 49% and 54% of documents
they should have produced, agreeing with each other just 28% of the time. Against
that baseline, legal-specific AI research tools now perform at or above human
accuracy on several measured tasks, even as general-purpose chatbot hallucination
remains a real and unevenly distributed risk depending on tool architecture.

The paper's second claim is distributional. Solo and small-firm practitioners are AI's
heaviest adopters — the force multiplier that lets one lawyer serve more clients at
lower cost — and, per California State Bar data, are demographically and
economically distinct from large-firm practice: younger, more likely to belong to
protected groups, and far less likely to carry malpractice insurance. A facially
neutral verification mandate that only large firms can absorb without cost therefore
operates as a mechanism of structural inequity, reachable under
rational-basis review, state disparate-impact statutes, and (more speculatively)
equal-protection doctrine's political-powerlessness rationale, even where the
federal *Washington v. Davis* intent requirement forecloses a direct constitutional
claim. Behavioral economics supplies the mechanism the doctrine misses: prospect
theory's asymmetric penalty structure, availability cascades following salient
hallucination cases, and algorithm aversion in a governing class demographically
insulated from the technology it regulates, together explain the overreaction better
than any account resting on cynical protection of the profession's monopoly.

The paper closes with three implementable, proportionate correctives — a
harmonized negligence standard applied consistently across malpractice,
court-sanctions, and disciplinary proceedings; safe-harbor provisions; and
evidence-based sanctioning thresholds calibrated to the tool, the task, and the stakes
of the matter — designed to let technological integration advance equity in the
profession rather than entrench a new tier within it.
