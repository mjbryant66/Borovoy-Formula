**Working title:** The AI Supervision Paradox: Double Standards, Algorithm Aversion, and the Unequal Burden of Legal Tech Regulation

**Proposed session:** Pro Se Litigation in the Age of Artificial Intelligence (works-in-progress)

---

A solo practitioner in Denton County, Texas downloads a draft factum from a
generative AI system: comprehensive, correctly identifying the controlling statute,
citing three on-point appellate decisions, and costing less than five dollars in
tokens. Under her county's standing order, she must now independently "verify"
every element of that factum "as accurate using traditional (non-AI) legal sources
by a human being." That verification takes as long as producing the factum without
AI would have, multiplying the five-dollar factum's true cost by an order of
magnitude. Across town, a mid-level associate at a large firm receives the identical
draft from the identical model, hands it to a junior associate to verify, and the
partner reviews the result in minutes — the verification burden absorbed by the
firm's pyramidal staffing structure without difficulty.

This project argues that the emerging judicial and regulatory response to
AI-assisted lawyering — a hardening "constructive recklessness" standard for
unverified AI-sourced citation error, contrasted with ordinary negligence for
human error — imposes this asymmetric burden without empirical justification, and
that pro se and under-resourced litigants sit on both sides of the resulting harm.
Solo and small-firm practitioners, disproportionately serving the clients who cannot
afford large-firm rates, are AI's heaviest adopters precisely because it is a force
multiplier: it lets one lawyer do more, at a price a self-represented litigant's
opposing counsel — or the litigant themselves — can afford. A verification mandate
calibrated to eliminate any productivity gain therefore has a direct access-to-justice
cost, quite apart from its effect on the individual practitioner.

The paper's empirical claim is that the double standard cannot survive scrutiny of
the underlying error rates. Human legal work is not the error-free baseline the
current doctrine implicitly assumes: independent reviewer teams in one influential
document-review study identified only 49% and 54% of the documents they should
have produced, agreeing with each other just 28% of the time. Against that record,
legal-specific AI research tools now perform at or above human accuracy on several
measured tasks, while general-purpose hallucination risk, though real, is unevenly
distributed by tool architecture rather than uniform across "AI" as a category. The
regulatory response has nonetheless moved toward treating any unverified AI
output as presumptively reckless — a standard the paper traces through court
sanctions orders, bar guidance, and disciplinary proceedings, and situates against
the psychology of algorithm aversion documented in the behavioral economics
literature: asymmetric penalty structures, availability cascades following salient
hallucination cases, and a governing class demographically insulated from the
technology it regulates.

Doctrinally, the paper works through the available frames for challenging this
asymmetry — federal equal protection (foreclosed in most applications by
*Washington v. Davis*'s intent requirement), Title VII (a statutory mismatch), and
state-law disparate-impact and rational-basis review, which the paper identifies as
the strongest fit, particularly where a state bar (California is the paper's primary
case study) maintains the kind of demographic self-reporting that makes a
disparate-impact showing tractable. For a works-in-progress session on pro se
litigation specifically, the project's access-to-justice throughline is direct: every
dollar added to the cost of AI-assisted representation is a dollar of unmet legal
need somewhere else in the system, and the current verification regime adds those
dollars precisely where the profession can least absorb them.

The paper closes with three implementable correctives — a harmonized negligence
standard applied consistently across malpractice, sanctions, and discipline;
safe-harbor provisions; and evidence-based sanctioning thresholds calibrated to
tool, task, and stakes — offered not as a deregulatory project but as the doctrine the
profession's own evidence already supports.

I would welcome the WIP session's feedback in particular on which doctrinal
pathway (state disparate-impact vs. rational-basis vs. a narrower professional-
responsibility argument) the panel finds most persuasive, and on how the
access-to-justice framing should be weighted against the equal-treatment framing
in the paper's remaining revisions.
