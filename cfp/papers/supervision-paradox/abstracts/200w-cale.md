**Title:** The AI Supervision Paradox: Double Standards, Algorithm Aversion, and the Unequal Burden of Legal Tech Regulation

A solo practitioner who uses generative AI to draft a factum is now told, by standing
order, to independently re-verify it using traditional, non-AI methods — a mandate
that erases the tool's time savings and often multiplies its true cost by an order of
magnitude. A large-firm associate handed the same draft hands verification down the
pyramid and absorbs it in minutes. This paper argues that the emerging regulatory
response to legal AI — a "constructive recklessness" standard for AI-sourced error,
paired with ordinary negligence for human error — imposes a structural inequity
rather than a neutral accuracy safeguard, and does so without empirical support: on
the current record, AI-assisted legal work performs at or above human baselines in
several measured domains, while human document review itself is far from
error-free. Because solo and small-firm practitioners are AI's heaviest adopters and
its most burdened, the doctrine falls disproportionately on demographically distinct,
resource-constrained lawyers and their clients. Drawing on constitutional
rational-basis and state disparate-impact analysis, and on the psychology of
algorithm aversion, the paper proposes a harmonized negligence standard, safe-harbor
provisions, and evidence-based sanctioning thresholds to replace the current
asymmetry.
