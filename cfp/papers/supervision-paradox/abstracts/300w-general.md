**Title:** The AI Supervision Paradox: Double Standards, Algorithm Aversion, and the Unequal Burden of Legal Tech Regulation

A solo practitioner in Denton County, Texas downloads a draft factum from a
generative AI system — comprehensive, correctly citing controlling authority, and
costing under five dollars. Under her jurisdiction's standing order, she must now
independently re-verify every element using traditional, non-AI legal research: hours
of work that erase the tool's savings and multiply the factum's true cost by an order
of magnitude. A mid-level associate at a large firm receiving the identical draft
hands verification to a junior associate; the partner reviews the result in minutes.
The verification burden the profession is imposing on AI-assisted work is
considerably more intensive than the supervisory model it already applies to
human-to-human work, and it falls hardest where it can least be absorbed.

This paper argues that the doctrinal response emerging in courts and bars — treating
unverified AI-sourced error as presumptively reckless while ordinary negligence
still governs human error — lacks empirical justification. The comparative evidence
does not support categorically distrusting AI output over human output: studies of
document review find agreement rates among human reviewers themselves below
30%, and legal-specific AI tools now perform at or above human baselines on several
measured tasks, even as general-purpose hallucination risk remains real and
uneven across tools. Because solo and small-firm lawyers are both AI's heaviest
adopters and the least able to distribute verification labor the way large firms do,
the current regime operates as a mechanism of structural inequity in professional
regulation, one plausibly reachable under state-law disparate-impact analysis even
where federal equal-protection doctrine is foreclosed. Behavioral economics —
prospect theory's asymmetric penalty structure, availability cascades, and algorithm
aversion — explains the overreaction better than any theory of cynical
guild-protection. The paper proposes three implementable correctives: a harmonized
negligence standard across malpractice, sanctions, and discipline; safe-harbor
provisions; and evidence-based sanctioning thresholds calibrated to tool and task.
